This has real legs. The part you're pointing at ("what has to happen for things to happen") is exactly what Civ-style games skip, and it's the most interesting part of real anthropology. Here's how I'd shape it.

## Core pillar: conditions, not unlocks

There's no tech tree. Each discovery has real preconditions that come together in the simulation, and when they do, it becomes *possible*, not guaranteed.

- **Pottery** needs clay nearby, controlled fire, and a reason to store things. Nomads don't haul heavy pots, so it also needs some settling down.
- **Agriculture** needs wild grasses with the right traits, plus people staying near the same patches over seasons, plus some pressure (drought, population) that makes the effort worth it.
- **Writing** needs surplus → specialists → someone keeping track of who owes what.

The player doesn't click "Research Bronze." They look at a society and wonder why it hasn't figured out metallurgy yet, and the answer is that there's no tin within 400km.

## Knowledge lives in people

This is the best anthro idea to steal. Technology isn't stored in a global progress bar. It lives in individuals and gets passed on by teaching and imitation. Two consequences:

- **You can lose tech.** If the only master flint-knapper dies in a flood, the skill can disappear. This really happened: Tasmania was cut off from mainland Australia and gradually lost several technologies.
- **Population and connectedness drive innovation**, not "science points." Small isolated groups stagnate and trading networks flourish. Joseph Henrich calls this the "collective brain."

## Domestication follows real rules

Not every animal can be domesticated. The animal needs a flexible diet, fast breeding, a calm temperament, and a herd hierarchy people can take over. Jared Diamond's point that zebras aren't horses, and that this shaped history, could be a mechanic. You can give a region wolves, but whether they become dogs depends on what the people do with them over generations.

## What are you? My pick: a god who's *misunderstood*

You can only act on the environment: weather, rivers, planting species, nudging animal traits, maybe a disease. You can't give orders. The societies watch what happens and **build theories about you**.

Say you send rain the day after they burned a goat. They notice the pattern and it turns into a ritual. Now they burn goats every drought, whether you respond or not. Their religion is their model of you, and it can be right, wrong, or hilariously superstitious. Religion then shapes behavior (food taboos, who holds power, whether they migrate), so your "gifts" have downstream effects you didn't intend.

Black & White (2001) touched on this, but only with one creature, not whole cultures.

## Where ML fits naturally (no forcing)

1. **Superstition is literally learning.** People noticing links between their actions and outcomes, including false ones, is just a learning algorithm. Each culture could run a small learner that tries to predict what the "god" does. Skinner's pigeons developed rituals exactly this way. This is the strongest fit, because the ML *is* the game mechanic.
2. **Cultural transmission as social learning.** Agents copy successful or high-status neighbors, with some error. That's a simple, well-studied model, and it produces fashions, traditions, and drift for free.
3. **Evolution.** Genetic algorithms on creature traits (and maybe the people's) are the obvious fit if you play with evolution.
4. **Emergent language (stretch goal).** There's research where agents invent their own words to coordinate. Neighboring tribes could end up with dialects that drift apart.
5. **LLMs: only for flavor.** They could write a chronicle or a myth text from the sim's event log. Never use them to run the simulation itself, or it becomes unpredictable and expensive.

## Games worth looking at

WorldBox (god sandbox), Dwarf Fortress's legends mode (emergent history), Creatures (neural-net creatures, 1996), Ancestors: The Humankind Odyssey, Species: ALRE (evolution sim), and RimWorld (a storyteller AI that paces events).

## How to start small

Don't build a world. Build **one valley, one band of ~30 people, one generation of time**. Give them fire, a few plants, one animal, and a drought. Get "they figured out food storage because winters were harsh and there was clay by the river" to emerge on its own. If that moment feels magical, you've got a game. Everything else scales out from there.

## Questions that change the design

- **Time scale:** do you watch individuals (RimWorld-close) or generations flashing by (WorldBox-far)? Maybe it zooms between the two?
- **One society or many?** Contact, trade, and conquest between cultures you shaped differently could be the endgame.
- **Is there a goal?** Or is it a sandbox where the "score" is the story your world tells?

You're right on both counts. Isaac isn't the tool for this, and the people should be abstract enough that their hands never "do" anything for real. Here's how the pieces fit.

## Why not Isaac or RL for the bodies

Isaac Lab trains physical motor control: balancing, walking, grasping with simulated joints. It's great for robots, and it's also why people in those demos look like wobbly ragdolls. For this game it would be:

- **Wrong layer:** the interesting question is *why* someone decided to knap flint, not *how* their fingers move.
- **Too expensive:** hundreds of agents living through generations at 100x speed can't each run a physics policy.
- **Uncontrollable:** you'd spend months getting someone to hold a rock.

So the body is **puppeted** and the mind is **simulated**. This is how The Sims, RimWorld, and Dwarf Fortress all work.

## Two layers, strictly separated

```
┌─────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│  SIMULATION (plain C#, no Unity dependency) │
│  agents, needs, knowledge, beliefs, world   │
│  ticks at any speed, can run headless       │
└──────────────────┬──────────────────────────┘
                   │ events: "Asha starts KNAP at rock#3, 40 min"
┌──────────────────▼──────────────────────────┐
│  PRESENTATION (Unity)                       │
│  picks animation, walks her there, plays    │
│  loop, spawns flakes particle, done         │
└─────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

The sim never knows animations exist. That means you can:

- Run 500 years headless in seconds to test whether agriculture ever emerges.
- Fast-forward in-game, where the presentation just skips or interpolates.
- Swap the art style later without touching the game logic.

## What an "agent" actually is

Each person is a bundle of data plus a decision loop:

- **Body:** age, health, hunger, fatigue, and simple heritable traits (strength, cold tolerance).
- **Personality:** a few sliders like curiosity, sociability, caution, and status-seeking.
- **Knowledge:** skills with a level, e.g. `knapping: 0.4`, `fire: 0.9`, `storage: 0.0`. Knowledge is per person, so if they die, it's gone.
- **Beliefs:** learned expectations like "gathering at the river in dry season → usually good" and "burning a goat → rain?".
- **Relationships:** kin, friends, and who they learn from.
- **Memory:** a short log of notable events, which also feeds the chronicle/myths later.

**Decisions use utility AI, not RL.** Every few in-game minutes, the agent scores the available **verbs** (gather, hunt, knap, cook, rest, socialize, teach, experiment, pray, migrate). Each score is roughly how badly they need it × how well they expect it to go × their personality. They pick one, probably with some randomness so they aren't robots. The Sims has run on essentially this model for 25 years, because it's cheap, readable, and debuggable.

## Where the ML actually sits

It sits in the **expectations**, not the bodies:

- **Each agent learns from experience.** After every action, they update what they expect it to produce in this context. That's a contextual bandit, basically a tiny Q-table per person. It's cheap and it's real learning.
- **Agents learn from each other.** They copy the expectations and skills of successful or high-status people, with copying errors. That's how culture, tradition, and superstition spread and drift.
- **The god-belief is the same machinery.** Your interventions are just another thing they try to predict.

This gives you non-scripted behavior at almost no compute cost. Unity ML-Agents *could* come in later for something narrow, like animal fleeing/herding behavior, but it's optional.

## Animation: a lookup, not ML

You don't need an animation-selector model. Each verb maps to a small set of clips:

| Verb | Clips |
|---|---|
| KNAP | sit + strike loop (3 variants) |
| GATHER | walk → crouch → pick loop → stand |
| SOCIALIZE | face partner, gesture/laugh loops |
| TEACH | same as the skill, plus a watcher playing "observe" |

A Unity Animator state machine or a simple C# script picks a clip from the verb, with a random variant. Hands only need to *look* right: IK snaps a prop to the hand bone, and a particle puff sells the flint strike. Mixamo or low-poly stylized characters are plenty.

## How it avoids feeling static

Static comes from **behavior** repeating, not from animation quality. What keeps this sim alive:

1. **Needs collide.** A hungry person who's also exhausted and grieving makes an odd choice, and you notice.
2. **Knowledge is uneven.** Only three people can make fire well, so everyone gathers around them. Social structure shows up on screen.
3. **Personality spreads outcomes.** Curious people waste time experimenting, and occasionally that's how pottery gets invented.
4. **The world changes:** seasons, herds migrating, a drought year.
5. **Cheap procedural polish:** agents look at whoever's talking, idles vary, walking paths spread out, kids follow parents. These are small touches, but they do most of the "alive" work.

## Level of detail for later

When you eventually zoom out to many societies, distant groups switch from individual agents to **aggregate stats** (population, skills distribution, beliefs). They switch back to individuals when you zoom in. Totally skippable for the one-valley prototype.

## One tick, concretely

> **Day 212, dry season.** Asha (curiosity 0.8, knapping 0.4) is hungry but the river roots came up empty the last two days, so her expected value for GATHER dropped. HUNT is low because she's never hunted. She scores EXPERIMENT high and picks it. She tries mixing river clay with ash near the fire and gets a 4% insight roll, which fails. Meanwhile Tomo watches her. He copies nothing because she didn't succeed, but her status drops slightly.
>
> **Presentation:** Asha walks to the fire, crouch-kneads a clay loop, and her thought bubble shows a question mark.

There are four tensions in your vision. They're the most important calls in the whole design, because they shape everything else, so I'm putting them first. For each one I give my suggestion, labeled as such, and you decide.

## 1. Destiny vs. pathways

Your founding question is whether people naturally discover certain things. Hidden tech pathways and an arc toward modernity partly answer that in advance.

- **Convergent:** everyone eventually gets to bronze → iron → steam. It differs only in speed and order.
- **Contingent:** some cultures never get metallurgy. They might go deep on astronomy, or plant medicine, or seafaring instead. "Modern" could look very different across islands.

*My suggestion:* **many paths up the same mountain, with a few dead ends.** There are hidden pathways, but several routes to each milestone, and some cultures may plateau for good unless contact or the god breaks the deadlock. That keeps your "does destiny exist?" question open *in play*, and players will argue about it.

## 2. How speculative is the biology?

Domestication and language can be very grounded. Amphibious and gliding humans are "what if" territory.

*My suggestion:* **grounded rules, speculative outcomes.** Traits only evolve under real selection pressure over real-ish time. So island people who dive for food over thousands of years could get webbed hands, better breath-holding, and seal-like fat. Each trait pathway is plausible on its own, even if the end result looks alien. The authenticity lives in the *process*.

## 3. Individuals vs. 50,000 years (the big one)

You want to know named people *and* reach the steam engine. A human life is about 40 years, and the stone age alone is tens of thousands of years.

*Options:*
- **Time flows at variable speed:** you fast-forward through quiet centuries and the game slows down for key moments (a discovery is close, a prophet is born, a disaster hits). People become a blur and then come into focus again.
- **Eras as chapters:** each era plays at human scale, then time jumps hundreds or thousands of years, with a summary of what happened in between.
- **Generational focus:** you follow a lineage, so your "favorite person" becomes their great-great-grandchildren.

*My suggestion:* **variable speed plus lineages.** It fits your "I'd been watching this guy" moments and the garden/puzzle/drama mix.

## Tension 1: the mind map, learned vs. legible

Your brain-map idea is the right place for ML, and it's a natural fit. The catch is that a deep neural net is a black box. You couldn't draw it as a readable mind map, you couldn't say why someone did something, and pre-birth shaping would have nothing clear to grab onto.

*My suggestion:* **the mind is a learned graph.**
- **Nodes** are readable things: `fire`, `river`, `the god`, `wolves`, `Asha`, `storing food`, `curiosity`.
- **Links** carry learned weights: "fire → warmth +0.9", "burning goats → rain +0.4 (wrong, but believed)", "Asha → trustworthy +0.7".
- **Learning** happens from experience (things that happen together get linked), from being taught, and from copying others. This is real, well-studied learning. It's just not deep learning.
- **Choices** come from scoring activities using the graph plus needs and personality.
- **The visual you wanted** falls straight out of this: a glowing web of a person's mind, with strong beliefs bright and fading ones dim. Current thoughts are whichever nodes are lit up right now.
- **Shaping** becomes concrete: pre-birth, you set trait nodes. At a branch point, you strengthen one link.

It's cheap enough to run for thousands of people, and every behavior is explainable. If we later want a neural piece for something narrow, it can plug in, but I wouldn't build the core on one.

## 1. How traits, interests, and lessons all shape decisions

Learned lessons are just one layer. A person's mind has several layers that change at different speeds:

| Layer | Examples | Changes | Where it comes from |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Traits** | curiosity, caution, sociability, patience, aggression, dexterity | almost never | genes + pre-birth shaping |
| **Interests** | stone, animals, plants, sky, spirits, people, water | slowly, over years | enjoyment, mentors, childhood experiences |
| **Skills** | knapping 0.6, tracking 0.2 | through practice | doing and being taught |
| **Beliefs / lessons** | "river roots fail in dry season," "the god likes fire" | constantly | experience, teaching, copying others |
| **Relationships** | trusts Asha, fears Tomo | constantly | interactions |
| **State** | hungry, grieving, afraid, inspired | minute to minute | body + events |
| **Culture** (shared) | taboos, customs, what's "proper" | across generations | the group's shared beliefs |

**When choosing an activity, each layer pulls differently:**

```
score = need it solves         (hunger makes FORAGE urgent)
      × expected result        (beliefs: "foraging at the river works")
      + interest bonus         (she likes plants, so it's rewarding in itself)
      + trait pulls            (curious → EXPERIMENT, cautious → avoids HUNT)
      + social pull            (friends are going; elder told her to)
      − culture                (taboo: nobody forages the burial grove)
      + a bit of randomness
```

**Traits also change how people learn**, which is where it gets interesting:
- **Cautious** people weight bad outcomes heavily, so one wolf attack makes them avoid the forest for years.
- **Stubborn** people barely update beliefs. They keep old superstitions longer, which is great for tradition and bad for progress.
- **Sociable** people copy others more. **Curious** people try untested things.
- **Interests grow from enjoyment.** A kid who loves stone and has a patient knapper grandfather becomes the specialist, and specialists are who trigger discovery tiles.

**Example: same situation, different people.** Winter, food is low.
- **Asha** (curious, likes plants) experiments with drying berries by the fire. → She could trigger *food preservation*.
- **Tomo** (bold, likes animals, good tracker) goes on a risky hunt.
- **Elder Ren** (cautious, stubborn, devout) organizes a ritual, because "last time we burned a goat, the snow stopped."

All three are reasonable choices from their minds, and the camp suddenly feels like real people. Does this layering match what you imagined?

## 2. Visuals and perspective (not decided, here are the trade-offs)

| Option | Feel | Cost / risk |
|---|---|---|
| **A. Full 3D free camera** (Sims, Planet Zoo) | most immersive close-ups | highest: animation, terrain, and camera all have to look good from every angle, which is where jank shows |

## 4. Family, reproduction, gender, aging, elaborated

These decide how a society *reproduces itself*, both biologically and culturally. My suggestion is to split them by what's fixed and what varies:

**Fixed biology (hard rules):**
- **Pregnancy, birth, infancy.** Long childhoods matter: kids need years of care, and that's when they absorb skills and beliefs. A culture that loses its elders loses its teachers.
- **Aging:** strength fades, but knowledge and status can grow. Elders become knowledge keepers, which ties into tech being lost when people die.
- **Fertility and mortality** respond to food, disease, and danger. Population growth comes out of the sim, not a slider.
- **Inheritance of traits:** kids blend their parents' traits with some mutation. This is what evolution runs on.

**Cultural variables (emerge as customs, different per society):**
- **Pairing:** monogamy, polygamy, arranged matches, loose bonds.
- **Kinship:** who counts as family, and whether lineage passes through the mother or the father.
- **Division of labor:** whether roles split by sex, by age, by skill, or not at all. This is **not hardcoded**. Real societies varied a lot, so it should emerge from pressures and customs.
- **Who raises children:** parents, grandparents, the whole band.

Why this split matters for gameplay: cultural variables shape **who learns what from whom**. That decides which skills survive and which discoveries happen. A culture where only men hunt and only women gather passes knowledge differently than one where everyone does everything. That's real anthropology turning into game mechanics.

Yes, eventually. But buildings in this game are trickier than in most, so how we handle them matters more than which pack you buy.

## Why one building pack won't cover it

1. **The range is huge:** from windbreaks and hide tents to stone temples, windmills, and steam-era factories.
2. **Cultures should look different.** A bamboo-and-thatch culture and a stone-and-hide culture shouldn't share a house model. Pandora-style cultures need their own architecture.
3. **People build, you don't.** Buildings come out of the sim (materials + knowledge + need), so they need **construction stages**, not just finished models.

## Proposed approach: building styles come from materials

Same idea as the discovery tiles. A building is a **type** (shelter, storage, workshop, shrine...) plus the **materials** that culture has available:

| | Wood + hide | Bamboo + thatch | Stone + mud |
|---|---|---|---|
| Shelter | hide tent | stilt hut | stone roundhouse |
| Storage | raised cache | woven granary | clay-lined pit |
| Shrine | carved post | flower altar | standing stones |

So architecture really does differ by culture, for the same reason the bows do. In assets, that means **modular kits** (walls, roofs, frames, supports) in several materials that we combine, rather than many unique buildings.

## For the first milestone: probably nothing to buy

Early structures are simple: fire pit, windbreak, lean-to, hide tent, drying rack, storage pit, maybe a kiln. Most of that can be **kitbashed from your nature packs** (logs, branches, rocks, leaves) plus a few hide and clay props. That's arguably more authentic, since early buildings literally were piles of nature.

## The catch: it's a subscription license

- ✅ A game that's **already released** can keep selling, and you can ship **minor bug fixes**.

| **Gather** | A hunter recruits others based on friendship, skill, and hunger | People grabbing spears, walking out together |
| **Track** | Find prey using tracking skill + beliefs ("deer at the river in summer") | Walking, crouching to read tracks, pointing |

**Animals run their own simple loop** too: graze, watch, flee, fight back. A boar can turn on the hunters, and wolves can show up at the kill. That's where the drama comes from: an injury, a death, a young hunter's first kill becoming a memory and a status boost.

**Group roles:** in a group hunt, people take roles by trait. Bold people strike, patient people drive the prey, kids watch and learn. Watching a hunt is literally how kids learn hunting in the sim.

So **yes, you can watch a whole hunt**, and the Kevin Iglesias pack covers this well (throwing, crouching, carrying, work loops). This is **Proposed**: every verb becomes a sequence of steps, not a single action.

## 2. Is there no more ML at all?

There is ML, it's just not neural networks:
- **The minds learn.** Beliefs update from prediction errors: expected X, got Y, adjust. That's reinforcement learning in its simplest real form.
- **Association learning.** Things that happen together get linked. That's where superstition comes from.
- **Social learning.** People copy successful people, with errors.
- **Evolution.** Inheritance plus selection is a genetic algorithm.

There are **no neural nets, no training phase, no GPU.** Every learner is small, runs live during play, and stays readable for the mind view.

**Two places a heavier ML piece could earn its spot later:**
1. **The prophet translator** (an LLM turns your typed words into commands). Already planned.
2. **Tuning the game.** Use optimization to search thousands of parameter combinations across many simulated runs, to hit the pass/fail tests (e.g. "pottery in 30–60% of runs"). It never ships in the game; it's a development tool that helps a solo dev balance a complex sim.

## 4. Time: three clocks, with a math problem to settle

Your three-clock idea is right, and it's the standard trick:

| Clock | What it governs |
|---|---|
| **Animation clock** | People always move at natural speed, so it never looks fast-forwarded |
| **Day/night clock** | The rhythm you see: sun, sleep, routines |
| **Calendar clock** | Seasons, aging, generations, evolution: what the sim actually runs on |

**The catch:** you said a Planet Zoo-style day/night of ~20 min *and* about 20 min per generation. A generation is ~25 years, so both can't be true if one visual day equals one week:

| If… | then 1 year = | season = | generation = |
|---|---|---|---|
| visual day (20 min) = 1 week | 17 hours | 4 hours | **~18 days of play** |
| generation = 20 min | 48 sec | 12 sec | 20 min |

**My suggestion: decouple day/night from the calendar completely.**
- The sun cycles slowly as ambience, maybe 10–20 min, and people's visible routines follow it (they sleep at visual night).
- Each visual day is a **representative day**. You see a natural-paced slice of village life, while the calendar underneath advances much more.
- **Seasons show gradually:** foliage, snow, and herds moving, not a calendar jump.
- **Generation ≈ 20 min at 1x**, with 1–3x speed like you said.
- When something important happens (a discovery close, a fork, a hunt you're following), the calendar **slows down** so you can watch it play out in full.

**Evolution needs separate handling.** Real visible changes like webbed hands take thousands of years, which at 20 min per generation is hundreds of hours. Two options:
- **Compressed evolution:** selection is exaggerated so traits shift noticeably within ~10–20 generations. Grounded rules, faster outcomes.
- **Deep-time skips:** an optional jump of centuries, with the sim running headless and a summary afterward.

Which feels right? Or both?
