This has real legs. The part you're pointing at ("what has to happen for things to happen") is exactly what Civ-style games skip, and it's the most interesting part of real anthropology. Here's how I'd shape it.

## Core pillar: conditions, not unlocks

There's no tech tree. Each discovery has real preconditions that come together in the simulation, and when they do, it becomes *possible*, not guaranteed.

- **Pottery** needs clay nearby, controlled fire, and a reason to store things. Nomads don't haul heavy pots, so it also needs some settling down.
- **Agriculture** needs wild grasses with the right traits, plus people staying near the same patches over seasons, plus some pressure (drought, population) that makes the effort worth it.
- **Writing** needs surplus → specialists → someone keeping track of who owes what.

The player doesn't click "Research Bronze." They look at a society and wonder why it hasn't figured out metallurgy yet, and the answer is that there's no tin within 400km.

## Knowledge lives in people

This is the best anthro idea to steal. Technology isn't stored in a global progress bar. It lives in individuals and gets passed on by teaching and imitation. Two consequences:

- **You can lose tech.** If the only master flint-knapper dies in a flood, the skill can disappear. This really happened: Tasmania was cut off from mainland Australia and gradually lost several technologies.
- **Population and connectedness drive innovation**, not "science points." Small isolated groups stagnate and trading networks flourish. Joseph Henrich calls this the "collective brain."

## Domestication follows real rules

Not every animal can be domesticated. The animal needs a flexible diet, fast breeding, a calm temperament, and a herd hierarchy people can take over. Jared Diamond's point that zebras aren't horses, and that this shaped history, could be a mechanic. You can give a region wolves, but whether they become dogs depends on what the people do with them over generations.

## What are you? My pick: a god who's *misunderstood*

You can only act on the environment: weather, rivers, planting species, nudging animal traits, maybe a disease. You can't give orders. The societies watch what happens and **build theories about you**.

Say you send rain the day after they burned a goat. They notice the pattern and it turns into a ritual. Now they burn goats every drought, whether you respond or not. Their religion is their model of you, and it can be right, wrong, or hilariously superstitious. Religion then shapes behavior (food taboos, who holds power, whether they migrate), so your "gifts" have downstream effects you didn't intend.

Black & White (2001) touched on this, but only with one creature, not whole cultures.

## Where ML fits naturally (no forcing)

1. **Superstition is literally learning.** People noticing links between their actions and outcomes, including false ones, is just a learning algorithm. Each culture could run a small learner that tries to predict what the "god" does. Skinner's pigeons developed rituals exactly this way. This is the strongest fit, because the ML *is* the game mechanic.
2. **Cultural transmission as social learning.** Agents copy successful or high-status neighbors, with some error. That's a simple, well-studied model, and it produces fashions, traditions, and drift for free.
3. **Evolution.** Genetic algorithms on creature traits (and maybe the people's) are the obvious fit if you play with evolution.
4. **Emergent language (stretch goal).** There's research where agents invent their own words to coordinate. Neighboring tribes could end up with dialects that drift apart.
5. **LLMs: only for flavor.** They could write a chronicle or a myth text from the sim's event log. Never use them to run the simulation itself, or it becomes unpredictable and expensive.

## Games worth looking at

WorldBox (god sandbox), Dwarf Fortress's legends mode (emergent history), Creatures (neural-net creatures, 1996), Ancestors: The Humankind Odyssey, Species: ALRE (evolution sim), and RimWorld (a storyteller AI that paces events).

## How to start small

Don't build a world. Build **one valley, one band of ~30 people, one generation of time**. Give them fire, a few plants, one animal, and a drought. Get "they figured out food storage because winters were harsh and there was clay by the river" to emerge on its own. If that moment feels magical, you've got a game. Everything else scales out from there.

## Questions that change the design

- **Time scale:** do you watch individuals (RimWorld-close) or generations flashing by (WorldBox-far)? Maybe it zooms between the two?
- **One society or many?** Contact, trade, and conquest between cultures you shaped differently could be the endgame.
- **Is there a goal?** Or is it a sandbox where the "score" is the story your world tells?

If you want, I can write this up as a proper design doc or build a tiny playable prototype of the one-valley sim. The superstition-learning mechanic would be a fun thing to test first.

You're right on both counts. Isaac isn't the tool for this, and the people should be abstract enough that their hands never "do" anything for real. Here's how the pieces fit.

## Why not Isaac or RL for the bodies

Isaac Lab trains physical motor control: balancing, walking, grasping with simulated joints. It's great for robots, and it's also why people in those demos look like wobbly ragdolls. For this game it would be:

- **Wrong layer:** the interesting question is *why* someone decided to knap flint, not *how* their fingers move.
- **Too expensive:** hundreds of agents living through generations at 100x speed can't each run a physics policy.
- **Uncontrollable:** you'd spend months getting someone to hold a rock.

So the body is **puppeted** and the mind is **simulated**. This is how The Sims, RimWorld, and Dwarf Fortress all work.

## Two layers, strictly separated

```
┌─────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│  SIMULATION (plain C#, no Unity dependency) │
│  agents, needs, knowledge, beliefs, world   │
│  ticks at any speed, can run headless       │
└──────────────────┬──────────────────────────┘
                   │ events: "Asha starts KNAP at rock#3, 40 min"
┌──────────────────▼──────────────────────────┐
│  PRESENTATION (Unity)                       │
│  picks animation, walks her there, plays    │
│  loop, spawns flakes particle, done         │
└─────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

The sim never knows animations exist. That means you can:

- Run 500 years headless in seconds to test whether agriculture ever emerges.
- Fast-forward in-game, where the presentation just skips or interpolates.
- Swap the art style later without touching the game logic.

## What an "agent" actually is

Each person is a bundle of data plus a decision loop:

- **Body:** age, health, hunger, fatigue, and simple heritable traits (strength, cold tolerance).
- **Personality:** a few sliders like curiosity, sociability, caution, and status-seeking.
- **Knowledge:** skills with a level, e.g. `knapping: 0.4`, `fire: 0.9`, `storage: 0.0`. Knowledge is per person, so if they die, it's gone.
- **Beliefs:** learned expectations like "gathering at the river in dry season → usually good" and "burning a goat → rain?".
- **Relationships:** kin, friends, and who they learn from.
- **Memory:** a short log of notable events, which also feeds the chronicle/myths later.

**Decisions use utility AI, not RL.** Every few in-game minutes, the agent scores the available **verbs** (gather, hunt, knap, cook, rest, socialize, teach, experiment, pray, migrate). Each score is roughly how badly they need it × how well they expect it to go × their personality. They pick one, probably with some randomness so they aren't robots. The Sims has run on essentially this model for 25 years, because it's cheap, readable, and debuggable.

## Where the ML actually sits

It sits in the **expectations**, not the bodies:

- **Each agent learns from experience.** After every action, they update what they expect it to produce in this context. That's a contextual bandit, basically a tiny Q-table per person. It's cheap and it's real learning.
- **Agents learn from each other.** They copy the expectations and skills of successful or high-status people, with copying errors. That's how culture, tradition, and superstition spread and drift.
- **The god-belief is the same machinery.** Your interventions are just another thing they try to predict.

This gives you non-scripted behavior at almost no compute cost. Unity ML-Agents *could* come in later for something narrow, like animal fleeing/herding behavior, but it's optional.

## Animation: a lookup, not ML

You don't need an animation-selector model. Each verb maps to a small set of clips:

| Verb | Clips |
|---|---|
| KNAP | sit + strike loop (3 variants) |
| GATHER | walk → crouch → pick loop → stand |
| SOCIALIZE | face partner, gesture/laugh loops |
| TEACH | same as the skill, plus a watcher playing "observe" |

A Unity Animator state machine or a simple C# script picks a clip from the verb, with a random variant. Hands only need to *look* right: IK snaps a prop to the hand bone, and a particle puff sells the flint strike. Mixamo or low-poly stylized characters are plenty.

## How it avoids feeling static

Static comes from **behavior** repeating, not from animation quality. What keeps this sim alive:

1. **Needs collide.** A hungry person who's also exhausted and grieving makes an odd choice, and you notice.
2. **Knowledge is uneven.** Only three people can make fire well, so everyone gathers around them. Social structure shows up on screen.
3. **Personality spreads outcomes.** Curious people waste time experimenting, and occasionally that's how pottery gets invented.
4. **The world changes:** seasons, herds migrating, a drought year.
5. **Cheap procedural polish:** agents look at whoever's talking, idles vary, walking paths spread out, kids follow parents. These are small touches, but they do most of the "alive" work.

## Level of detail for later

When you eventually zoom out to many societies, distant groups switch from individual agents to **aggregate stats** (population, skills distribution, beliefs). They switch back to individuals when you zoom in. Totally skippable for the one-valley prototype.

## One tick, concretely

> **Day 212, dry season.** Asha (curiosity 0.8, knapping 0.4) is hungry but the river roots came up empty the last two days, so her expected value for GATHER dropped. HUNT is low because she's never hunted. She scores EXPERIMENT high and picks it. She tries mixing river clay with ash near the fire and gets a 4% insight roll, which fails. Meanwhile Tomo watches her. He copies nothing because she didn't succeed, but her status drops slightly.
>
> **Presentation:** Asha walks to the fire, crouch-kneads a clay loop, and her thought bubble shows a question mark.

## Suggested path

1. **Headless sim first, maybe 1–2 weeks.** One valley on a grid, ~30 agents, 6–8 verbs, seasons, 3 discoverable techs (fire mastery, storage, pottery). Output a log plus charts. The only question it answers is whether emergence ever feels surprising.
2. **Unity with capsules.** Hook it to the event stream using colored capsules and floating icons. This is where you tune whether watching it is fun.
3. **Real art and animation** only after step 2 is fun.

For step 1 I could build a quick version right now, either a browser demo you can watch or a plain C# library you'd drop straight into Unity later. The C# option wastes less work since Unity is the target.

## Why skip Unity for now

The first question is whether the sim produces surprising emergence, and answering it means running hundreds of years in seconds, rerunning with different seeds, and reading logs and charts. Unity slows that loop down with domain reloads, play mode, and scene setup. A terminal app runs in about a second.

The trick is to write the sim **so that Unity can use it later unchanged**.

## The stack

| Thing | Choice | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Language | **C#** | Same language as Unity, so nothing gets rewritten |
| Runtime | **.NET SDK 8 or 10** | Builds and runs the sim from the terminal |
| Core library target | **netstandard2.1** | The format Unity 6 can load directly |
| Editor | **VS Code + C# Dev Kit**, or **JetBrains Rider** (free for non-commercial use) | Rider is excellent with Unity later |
| Version control | **Git** (already installed) | Commit early, since experiments will break things |
| Output | **CSV logs + a simple HTML chart viewer** | Lets you see population, skills, and beliefs over time |

## Project layout

```
Ideas and Thoughts/
└── Anthro/
    ├── Anthro.Sim/        ← core library (netstandard2.1): agents, world, verbs, learning
```
