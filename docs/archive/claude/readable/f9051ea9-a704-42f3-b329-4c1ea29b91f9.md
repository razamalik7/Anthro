## Weather within the map

Yes, that's what drives migration. In the sim, deer move between summer and winter ground on a fixed calendar. In reality they move because conditions differ across the land:
- high ground gets deep snow and greens up late, so deer winter in the valleys and follow the green-up uphill in spring;
- one valley has a dry year while the next is lush, and herds drift toward the better grazing.

The map already has elevation, so this can come out of real causes:
- temperature drops with height (about 6.5 °C per km);
- snow lies deeper on high ground;
- spring arrives later on hills;
- rain varies from region to region, differently each year.

Plants grow according to their local conditions, and animals go where food is good and snow is shallow. Migration then happens because of the land, not a calendar, and people have to follow the herds or switch foods.

## Off-map: open world, no simulated outside hunting

Agreed. Herds coming and going across the edge is how people experienced it. We're not an island.

On your question "shouldn't hunting shrink the herd?" It does shrink: the off-map deer fell from about 39,000 to about 32,000. Then it stops shrinking, because a thinned herd has more food per animal and breeds faster. That's real ecology, and it's why real hunting rarely wiped out common deer. The herd settles where the births match the kills.

Plus **the body that feels being lean**, which you're on board with.

## Extinction

Permanent, then. One consequence worth knowing: if the wolves die out, no band can domesticate dogs from then on, because the first dog step needs a wolf pack nearby. That's a nice emergent result.

## Dog domestication

It's there, as four discoveries in a chain:

| Stage | What it represents | What it does in the sim now |
|---|---|---|
| `dog.lurk` | Wolves hanging around camp for scraps (needs wolves within ~5 km) | Wolves are 1.3× more wary of attacking the camp |
| `dog.pup` | A pup raised in camp (needs wolves around and someone interested in animals) | 1.1× more |
| `dog.camp` | Camp dogs, kept over generations | 1.5× more, and the camp is likelier to wake to a night raid |
| `dog.hunt` | Hunting dogs | Hunting skill +0.2 |

**The weakness:** dogs are only knowledge, not animals. A band that "knows" camp dogs keeps them forever without feeding them, and the dogs never breed or die. Real dogs were animals people had to feed, and they could be lost in a famine. A proper version would give each band a dog pack that eats scraps and meat, breeds, guards the camp, helps hunts and carries loads, and dies in hard times. That fits the closed-world ecology we just built. I'd put it on the list after this food pass.

## How much discoveries mattered

So far the evidence says not much, and not in a good way.

1. **Returns per hour barely moved.** In the 12-world food audit, hunting stayed around 0.33 rations an hour and foraging 0.4–0.6 from year 10 to year 60, even as bows, traps, nut processing and drying spread. The hunting boom around years 20–40 came from people learning that hunting pays, on top of the endless off-map herds, not from new weapons.
2. **Everything spread everywhere.** By year 60, about 40 discoveries were known by about 85% of everyone in every band. That leaves no technological differences between bands, and no band having something another lacks, which takes away much of what makes discoveries interesting.
3. **Earlier one-at-a-time tests** (handing a discovery to everyone and comparing):
   - throwing spears cut starvation from 47% to 32% of deaths;
   - cooking and food preservation made little difference to population (about 280 vs 285 people).

   But those were measured back when the eating bug distorted everything.
4. **82 of the 103 stages do something directly in the code.** That's coverage, not impact: many effects are 1.1× or 1.15× multipliers that disappear into the noise.

**My read:** with food unlimited, discoveries couldn't matter. Nobody was short, so a better spear or a drying rack changed nothing. Once food is really limited by closed herds, lean winters, masts and runs, discoveries should start to count, because a band that dries fish or boils bone grease gets through a winter that another band doesn't.

## How hunting was fixed

It took nine rounds of test runs, because there were five separate causes. Each fix uses a real mechanism rather than a tuned number:

1. **Food work no longer looks worth doing when food is piled up.** Extra food is only worth what can be eaten before it spoils, and that now counts all food in camp.
2. **Hunting parties count meat already in camp** when judging whether a kill is worth it.
3. **The meat craving follows diet.** People crave meat when it's been under about 10% of what their family eats, and stop craving it around 40%. Before, the craving was nearly always on, because warm-weather meat rots in four days.
4. **Searching for game takes real time.** A party that finds nothing walks on for hours, so when game is scarce whole days come back empty and people switch to other food.
5. **Hunted herds learn to be shy of people:** heavily hunted animals keep their distance for a year or two.

The no-people test of the land also exposed five problems in the animals themselves, now fixed:
- **Wolf kills overlapped natural deaths.** Wolves mostly take animals that were about to die anyway, but here every kill was extra.

The picture at year 45 in one world: deer were holding at about 5,000 while people took 600–970 a year (about 5 per person). Then a hard winter starved about 1,200 deer, wolves took about 870, people kept hunting, and the herd collapsed.

I've made a lot of changes since your last message, and this has become a design decision rather than a bug. Here's where things stand.

## Item 1, famine: the structural fixes worked
- **Sharing in a crisis:** families with food now share down to the hungry family's level, as foragers' demand sharing does, instead of keeping everything under 2 days' worth.
- **No more microbands:** hard times no longer splinter bands into groups of 2–4. Small groups join bands they know, and splits are now 6–14 people.
- **Inner bark** as a lean-season fallback food. It's slow to gather, and the woods near camp get stripped.
- **Result:** starvation fell from about 38% of deaths to about 15% over the first 30 years.

## The new problem: nothing slows growth until famine and game collapse
With winter survivable, populations grow about 2% a year, reaching 170–350 by year 60. Births per woman are about 6–7, Hadza-like; the real-world fertility curve and secondary sterility I added barely changed that.

The only brakes come late and hard:
- **famine** again, at 25–67% of deaths in years 30–60;
- **deer extinction** in 7 of 12 worlds.

The arithmetic: this map's game can sustainably feed about 110 people eating only meat, or about 220 eating half meat, after the wolves' share. Once people pass that, the deer go. Real foragers at these densities (0.1–0.2 per km²) did live alongside deer. They had more kinds of game (elk, aurochs, beaver, birds) and more fish, and when hunting got poor they ate more plants or moved on, long before the game was gone.

## Options
- **A. Richer real fauna.** Add more species of the right kind for a temperate valley: red and roe deer, aurochs or bison, beaver, waterfowl, and stronger fish runs. More total game, spread across prey with different habits. Grounded, but a bigger job.
- **B. Hunting effort that falls with scarcity.** Hunters currently share every sighting through gossip, so the last herds still get found. Sightings could go stale faster, or tell less. Smaller and more targeted.
- **C. Births responding before famine.** Fertility currently only drops once people are thin. Real women's fertility also falls under steady hard work and long foraging days, which would slow growth before famine hits.
- **D. Accept it.** Bands boom, overshoot, and crash with famine and game loss. That's arguably how many real populations behaved, and it's very much "survive, not thrive". But losing all the deer in more than half of worlds seems too much.

**My recommendation is B + C first** (smaller and grounded), then A later as an ecology upgrade.

Four 150-year worlds are running in the background to show where the ceiling actually ends up; I'll share them when they finish. Which way do you want to go?

### Claude (Assistant)

Taking these in order.

## 1. Splitting
I agree. The point of a size limit is that bigger bands should need better technology. That's how it worked for real foragers: a band of about 25 is roughly what can feed itself within a day's walk of camp, and bigger groups needed things like storage, fish weirs, wild grain, or ways to keep peace among many people.

That isn't what drives splits right now. Most splits are **"bad blood"**: grudges pile up and a band breaks apart every few years whatever its size. In one 20-year run there were 65 splits and 400+ family moves, with bands averaging 10–14 people. So neither the player nor the bands learn "we need technology to grow"; they just churn.

What I'd change, all emergent:
- **Splits come from what size really costs.** As a band grows, the land near camp gets stripped, so people walk farther for less. That pressure builds and pushes a split. Better technology (storage, fishing gear, grain, dogs) raises the size a camp can feed.
- **Big groups mean more friction,** since people know fewer of the others. Shared customs and rituals already ease this in the code. Leadership or ritual discoveries would ease it more, which is the path toward villages and towns.
- **Grudges cause fewer whole-band splits.** Real foragers mostly resolved feuds by one family moving to another band, not by splitting.

## 2. Inner bark
It's real. Scots pine inner bark was ground into famine bread in Finland and Sweden into the 1800s. The Mohawk name for the Adirondack people means "bark eaters". Archaeologists find bark-peeled pines thousands of years old in Scandinavia and British Columbia. But it was a famine food, and you don't have to like it.

It also didn't work out: by getting people through winter, it removed the brake on growth. I'd **take it out**, or at most make it a discovery. That fits your "tech lets bands grow" idea: a band that learns it gets a famine buffer.

## 3. Nobody eats only meat
That was just my arithmetic: the game on this map could feed about 110 people if they ate nothing else. In the runs, hunting supplies about 20–60% of food and plants the rest.

## 4. Hunting: is it a real tracking system?
Mostly, yes:
- Every herd has a **real position** and moves each day by its own choices.
- Each person keeps their **own last-seen sighting** of herds they've personally seen within about 1 km, or heard about.
- A hunt heads to the **believed location** and has to **track** from there. The chance of catching up falls with how far the herd has moved since it was seen, and rises with skill and dogs. Following the trail closes the gap. A party with no sightings now roams and searches.

Its weak spots:
- **Gossip passes exact coordinates.** Sightings spread through camp talk, so everyone knows where every herd was seen in the last 3 days. That's what lets people find the last few herds.
- **There's a small knowledge leak:** a party's list of targets silently drops herds that have died or left the map, so people "know" things they couldn't.
- **Tracks aren't physical.** There are no trails or footprints left on the land, and snow doesn't help with tracking (in reality fresh snow makes it far easier). Tracking is just a dice roll on distance and skill.

A proper version would have herds leave **tracks on the cells they cross**, fading over days, much faster in rain and lasting longer in snow. Hunters would find and follow fresh tracks, and second-hand news would be vaguer ("deer by the north marsh a few days ago"), not exact coordinates. That's real tracking, and it naturally makes scarce game hard to find.

## Proposed order
1. Remove inner bark, or make it a discovery.
2. Rework splitting around what size costs, plus friction among many people, with technology raising the limit.
3. Physical tracks and vaguer sighting news, which should also stop the deer wipeouts.
4. Then re-check famine, births and growth.

Do you want bark removed, or made a discovery?

## How the autumn stocking-up works

People carry a learned sense of **how lean the winters are**, built from how hungry they actually were in past winters and springs and spread through talk. From late summer through autumn, each person compares their family's **keeping food** (nuts, grain, dried food) with how much they expect the lean months to need. Civil wars can still happen. The fix doesn't forbid violence within a people; it changes which learned expectations people use.

**How it works now:**
- Every person carries learned expectations about what hitting someone brings, separately for kin, friends, neutral acquaintances, people they distrust, and outsiders.
- **Each people starts with its own culture.** Its baseline violence expectation is drawn at random, so some peoples start more violent and others more peaceful, and the gap between how they treat insiders and outsiders also varies.
- **Cultures drift with experience.** If attacks pay off, people learn violence works, and it spreads through watching and gossip. If they backfire, the taboo strengthens.
- Before tonight's fix, everyone in another camp counted as an outsider, so kin camps used the weakest taboo and went to war within a year. Now members of another camp of your people are judged by your real relationship with them.

**So a civil war can grow naturally.** Camps squabble over shared ground, grudges and distrust build, and people come to distrust the other camp. Distrust carries a weaker taboo than kinship or friendship, so violence gets easier and raids can follow. Long bad blood is also the biggest part of what drives a **schism**: the feuding camp breaks away as a new people. Then they really are outsiders to each other, and wars between peoples follow the usual rules. That's a nice emergent arc: feud, civil war, split, rival peoples.

**One thing I did hard-code** and should fix: a raid against a camp of your own people is always weighed as if against "neutral" acquaintances. It should depend on how the raider actually feels about that camp, so a camp they've come to distrust is easier to attack than one full of friends. Fixing it now.

**Monogamy is hard-coded right now,** and you're right that it doesn't need to be. Each person has a single partner slot, and pairing only happens between two unpartnered people. Real foragers varied: most marriages were monogamous, some men had two wives (often 5–15% of men in forager societies; the !Kung and Hadza had some), and the practice depended on culture and circumstance. It was commoner where men were scarce, as in our small worlds after raids. Some peoples also had widow inheritance or occasional polyandry.

A non-hard-coded version would let a person take a second partner when both want it. It would be weighed against the existing partner's jealousy (already in the sim as an innate reaction) and against a **learned custom of the people**, which forms from what people actually do, like the existing custom about who moves at marriage. Some peoples would then drift toward allowing plural marriage and others away from it, and a shortage of men would naturally push in that direction. I'll add it to the queue.

**Gender roles are mostly emergent, not hard-coded.** What is built in is biological:
- women carry, give birth and nurse;
- late pregnancy and nursing an infant make a long hunt costly, which steers nursing mothers away from hunting;
- bodies differ by sex on average (strength, build).

What isn't built in: there's no "men hunt, women gather" rule. Who hunts, forages, fishes or cooks comes from each person's strength, interests, skills and situation, so a division of labour emerges from those, as anthropologists think it did. The customs on who moves at marriage and who keeps the children after a breakup are learned from what people actually do. The remaining sex-specific rules are biological: women can only conceive until about 45, men can partner until 60.

**On the lone wanderers:** the chronicle shows strike-outs are whole families (2–6 people), 8 in 15 years, not single men, so they're not what's lowering births. The bigger signal in that world is **68 killings in 15 years**. Violence is cutting into the men, which is what leaves young women unpaired. I'll check whether that's typical across the 12 worlds once the run with the latest fix finishes.

## Should marriage be hard-coded?

**No.** Here's how I'd draw the line: **hard-code the biology, let the institution be learned.**

**What humans actually do**
- **Polygyny** (one man, several wives) is allowed in about 85% of recorded societies. Even there, most marriages are monogamous, because few men can support two families. Among foragers, maybe 5–20% of men have a second wife. It gets much more common once wealth can be stored or inherited: herds, land, granaries.
- **Polyandry** (one woman, several husbands) is rare, around 1%. It shows up where land is scarce, as in Tibet, where brothers share one wife so the family farm isn't split.
- **"Partible paternity"** is a belief in some Amazonian groups that a child can have several fathers. Each man who slept with the mother helps provide for the child, and those children survive better.
- **Kids raised collectively** is actually the human baseline. Grandmothers, aunts, older siblings and other adults do a large share of the childcare, so that's not a stretch at all. The Mosuo in China go further: there is no marriage, and children are raised by the mother's family while fathers only visit.
- **"Only the king mates"** never happens biologically in humans. But extreme versions of it do happen through power: harems and despotic chiefs, with a handful of men fathering a huge share of children. That's something power produces, not a mating system.
- **Queen-only breeding** (like ants or bees) is outside humanity. It would take a different species over millions of years.

**What I'd build**
1. **Hard-code only the biology:**
   - people form attachment bonds;
   - jealousy exists;
   - men can't be sure a child is theirs;
   - pregnancy and nursing cost the mother;
   - children need years of provisioning.
2. **Make the rest learned customs, using the same expectation system** that already drives sharing and violence:
   - Is taking a second partner approved, tolerated, or punished?
   - Do children belong to the mother's family or the father's?
   - Does the whole camp feed children?
3. **Let the pressures choose the outcome:**
   - A woman (or her family) chooses between being a good provider's second wife and a poor provider's only wife. Where providers are very unequal, polygyny emerges. Where everyone is roughly equal, it stays rare.
   - When storage and inheritance appear, inequality rises and so does polygyny.
   - Where land is scarce, brothers sharing one wife can emerge.
   - Despotic polygyny comes from power, through the existing violence and status systems.
4. **Evolution paths (optional, slow):** if bonding strength and jealousy were heritable traits, mating psychology could drift over thousands of years. That's real evolution, but it would be invisible on a timescale of a few centuries. Nothing outside the human range would be possible.

This also fits the god angle: a god nudging a people toward or away from these customs creates real drama, like a jealous husband, a chief who hoards wives, or a camp that raises its orphans together.

**The question for you:** should mating psychology be heritable (an evolution path), or fixed biology with only the customs varying? I'd go with customs only for v1 and add heritable traits later.

## Feasting and rationing: learned or hard-coded?

**Feasting was a hard-coded rule I wrote earlier:** "with more than 3 days of food at home, eat 150% of need." Nothing was learned. The rule is right for fresh meat after a big kill, and real foragers did gorge then: the meat would rot otherwise, and eating it turns it into body fat, which is the best storage there is. It's wrong for winter nuts, which keep for months. So the rule itself was broken, not the world.

**How it interacts with spoilage:** the rule ignored spoilage, so stores that would have lasted got eaten early. I've now tied it to spoilage. People eat past need only when:
- they have more fresh meat and greens than the family can eat in about 2 days, before it rots; or
- they have keeping food beyond what the lean stretch calls for.

That follows from how fast each food spoils, which is already a fixed fact in the game. It isn't a culture rule.

**Rationing is a fixed decision rule fed by learned knowledge**, which is the same pattern as the rest of the game:
- **Fixed:** "while the land isn't keeping up, spread what's in store over the time you expect it to have to last." It's simple arithmetic, like a hunter choosing which deer to stalk.
- **Learned:** how long and how lean the hard stretch is, from people's own memories of getting thin in each season, which spread by talk. A people that has never had a bad spring won't ration, and gets caught out once. Survivors learn, and the lesson passes to their children and neighbours.
- **Hand-set:** the floor of half portions.

If you want it more cultural, rationing strictness could become a learned custom: people who feasted and then starved learn to ration harder, and people who rationed and then watched food rot learn to loosen up. I'd hold that for now unless you want it.

## The two plural-marriage changes are in (running as v33)

1. **Children are counted by whose they are.** Each person in a plural marriage learns from their own children in it. A man with two wives has more children; a woman with two husbands doesn't, and each of her husbands has fewer. Nothing tells the game that polygyny pays and polyandry doesn't; it follows from who bears the children.
   - Onlookers judge by people like themselves. Men watch how the husband does, women watch how the wives do. Men and women can now come to see the custom differently.
2. **Jealousy scales with what's at stake for the body:**
   - **A man can't be sure a child is his,** so sharing a wife puts his offspring at stake.
   - **A woman's children are certainly hers,** so what she risks is her husband's food and care, which counts more the better he provides.
   - It also scales with the share of the spouse each person loses. Going from one spouse to two hurts more than going from two to three.

v33 also includes cross-camp food sharing and the spoilage-based feasting rule. It's running now: 10 worlds × 40 years.

## The collapses: two different ways down

**1. Violent cultures (worlds 6, 8, 10)**
- These lost 28–46 people to killing in the first decade, out of 50–70. That's **5–7% of the population killed per year.** The most violent real forager societies on record ran around 0.5–1% per year.
- The killings are mostly inside a camp: "killed by Torish", "killed by Dal", repeat killers.
- Births also collapse in those years (21–28 per decade against 45–64 in calm worlds), because the dead are young adults and feuds break up families.
- Once the population is small, the killing stops and they stabilise around 30–40.

You said violence varying between worlds is fine, and the variation itself is good. But at this level a violent culture tears itself apart within 5 years, which breaks "humans persist without a god." I'd tone down the extreme end without removing it, so violent peoples bleed rather than self-destruct.

**2. Famine spirals (worlds 4, 7)**
- **World 7:** five bad springs in the first decade killed 65 people (72 → 30). With so few left, births fell to about 1 a year. Then one more bad decade left 10 people.
- **World 4:** famine every decade (29, 21, 32, 16 deaths) despite knowing a lot, including cold storage and hearths. Births fell decade by decade, from 45 to 10. Starving women stop conceiving, which is the brake you picked, but it compounds into a death spiral.

Neither world was short of food over the year: they produced 1.15–1.35 times what they needed. It all comes down to the spring gap.

## Carrying capacity: are we where we want to be?

- **Density:** the booming worlds level off around 150–165 people on the 1,600 km² map, about **0.1 people per km²**. Real temperate foragers ran about 0.05–0.3 per km², so this is plausible, toward the sparse end.
- **What limits them is right:** not total food, but the worst season. At the cap they still produce about 1.25 times their yearly need and throw a lot away. **Spoiled food equals 40–98% of what gets eaten**, mostly summer and autumn gluts that nobody preserves. Real seasonal foragers lived exactly this way, bottlenecked by late winter and spring, and storage was what raised their numbers.
- **This sets up the tech lever well:** better storage → surviving the spring gap → a higher ceiling. That's the "tech lets bands get bigger" path you wanted, and it shows. The two worlds that know the most (9 and 5) boomed, and the three that know the least (8, 10, 7) collapsed.
- **What's off:** world 4 learned a lot and still spiralled, so knowing isn't enough unless people actually use it at scale. That's worth a look.

## Splits and camps (the player-management concern)

- **Splitting:** 12–34 splits per world over 40 years, so up to about one a year in growing worlds. People also move between camps constantly: 25 to 270 individual moves over 40 years.
- **Camps:**
  - 2–4 in small worlds;
  - **8–13 in booming ones**, many tiny (final sizes like 47/32/28/18/14/5/2/1).
- **Peoples:** always 1. No schism ever happened in 40 years.

The fluid camp membership is realistic; forager bands constantly reshuffle. But your v1 target was 1–2 camps you really play with and 3–5 at most. Booming worlds still blow well past that. This is still open, and I'd raise it as a design choice: let camps of one people merge back more readily, or treat small satellite camps as part of the main one for the player.

## Discoveries

- **Pace:** 7–20 stages known by year 40. Without a god, that might be **too fast** for "advance slowly." These stages (spear-thrower, cordage, hearths, cold storage) took real humans thousands of years each. Worth deciding what pace you want; the discovery rate is still your call.
- **Impact:** strong. It's the clearest predictor of which worlds thrive.
- **Knowledge gets lost:** in world 9, storytelling went from 84% to 12% of people as knowers died and camps split. Torch-making fell from 100% to 73% in world 6. That's realistic, and good drama for a god to step into.
- **Dogs:** world 9 reached the "wolves lurk near camp" stage (67% knew it), but nobody went on to taming pups, so there are **0 dogs anywhere**. That's the next stage, and it's by design. My earlier "231 dogs" was a misread column; that was people moving between camps.

## Ecology and animals

Very stable and healthy:
- **Deer:** 4,700 → 5,500–6,000.
- **Boar:** about 2,000 → 2,700.
- **Rabbits:** 8,200 → 14,000, filling up to capacity from the 80% start.
- **Wolves:** steady at 25–38.
- **Mammoths:** migratory, so they're off the map when counted. They still kill 4–9 people per world when they pass through.

Human hunting takes only 2–6% of the deer a year, so people barely dent the herds. That's realistic at 0.1 people per km². **One exception:** in world 5, people learned to hunt boar heavily in the last years (1,236 boar in year 40) and the boar population fell from 2,850 to 1,550. That's the first real human-driven decline, and it emerged entirely on its own. Something to watch.

## Seasons and climate

- **Spring is the one real bottleneck:** 0–6 bad springs per decade depending on the world. Bad winters are rare now that people store and ration.
- Weather varies by year and by place across the map; some worlds just get luckier runs.

## Behaviour

- **Work:** 3.9–5.4 hours of food-getting per adult per day, in line with real foragers (the Hadza are around 4–6).
- **Leisure:** 3–4.4 hours of socialising, 1–2.7 of rest. A lot, but foragers do socialise a lot.
- **Diet:** meat is 12–67% of food depending on the world. That's the real forager range, and it varies by culture and land.
- **Lifespan:**
  - Life expectancy at birth: 30–39 in good worlds, which is spot on for foragers. Only 8–14 in collapsing ones.
  - Most deaths are infants dying of lung sickness and starvation. Respiratory infection really is the top forager killer.

- **Pairing:** mostly fine. Small worlds show odd gaps, like no women aged 25–34 partnered, but those are a handful of people.
- **Plural marriage (before the biology change):**

  - Wives with several husbands were as common as husbands with several wives. v33 should fix that.
