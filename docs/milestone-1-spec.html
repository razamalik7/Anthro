02 · World & ecology

A valley that pushes back

A grid of about 64 × 64 cells, each roughly 50 m across, so around 3 km square. Big enough to have a far side worth walking to. Proposed

FeatureWhy it's there
River + marshWater, fish, reeds for fiber and baskets, a clay bank
ForestWood (some springy, some hard), nuts, game, wolves
GrasslandDeer herds, wild grasses with edible seed
HillsFlint outcrop, a cave, ochre

Animals

Deer (herds, move with the seasons), rabbit, boar, river fish, a wolf pack, and one megafauna species that can be hunted out for good. All from the polyperfect packs. Proposed

Ecology rules Decided

  • Plant patches regrow by season. Overharvest a patch and it comes back slower.
  • Animal populations grow toward a limit, get eaten by predators, and crash if overhunted.
  • Wolves follow the deer. Fewer deer means hungrier, bolder wolves.
  • Seasons: four. Winter is the main pressure. Weather is temperature + rainfall per season, nothing fancier.

How a choice gets made

      + interest bonus        (enjoys this domain)

pick from the top few, weighted

How learning works

  • From experience: after an action, the belief moves toward what actually happened. Surprise moves it more. Caution makes bad outcomes count double; stubbornness slows all change.
  • From others: people copy beliefs and skills from those who seem successful or high-status, plus kin. Sociable people copy more. Copies come with small errors, which is how traditions drift.
  • From coincidence: two things that happen close together get linked, true or not. This is where superstition comes from.
  • Culture: when most of the band holds a belief strongly, it hardens into a custom or a taboo that pushes on everyone.

Inheritance and pairing Decided

Children get the average of both parents' traits plus random drift, with an occasional bigger jump. Pairs form from relationship, attraction and availability, never close kin. Attraction is partly cultural: a band that prizes good hunters or skilled hands spreads those traits faster. Physical trait pathways (fur, webbing) come in later milestones.

Mindexperiment, explore, ritual / pray, mourn

Verbs are sequences you can watch Decided

A verb is a chain of steps, each with its own odds and animation. A hunt runs: gather a party → track → stalk → strike → chase or finish → butcher & carry → share. Animals run their own loop (graze, watch, flee, fight back), so a boar can turn on the hunters and wolves can show up at the kill. Children come along and learn by watching.

Experiment is the engine of discovery. A curious person picks something near them (a material, a tool, a place) and tries something new. When all of a tile's conditions are met, experiments in the right domain get a chance to trigger it.

05 · Discovery web

Skills made of stages

Each row is a skill. Each box is a stage: a real discovery with its own needs, its own effects, and sometimes an accident that can trigger it. Stages sit left to right by how many steps they are from the start. Click a stage to light up everything it depends on and everything it opens; click a skill name to see the whole skill. Decided (v1, open to tuning)

known at start one of several routes can happen by accident italic skill = belief or way of life

Rules every stage follows Decided

  • Normal discovery: all needs met, plus a qualifying person working or experimenting in that area, plus a lucky roll.
  • Accident: only the physical needs (materials and the situation). Someone nearby has to notice, or you omen them toward it. The result is an insight and beginner skill, not mastery.
  • Use it or lose it: a stage lives in people. It spreads by teaching and fades without practice, so an accident that doesn't fit the band's way of life dies out.
  • Routes leave a mark: a band that only has sinew thread sews well but never nets fish; both routes together open combination stages.
06 · God & belief

Your six hands, and what they cost

The omen is your main hint tool: you can't tell anyone to try the clay, but you can make the clay bank impossible to ignore. Proposed

Favor and reputation Decided

  • Favor is what you spend. It comes from acts of belief: prayer (steady), rituals (bursts), offerings, and sacrifice (big, and it pushes your reputation toward cruel).
  • Prayers are requests. Answer one and belief grows; ignore it and doubt grows. Rain that falls on its own after a prayer still counts for you.
  • Your pool is capped by how many believe, and belief fades without signs.
  • Reputation, kind to cruel, is never spent. Kind makes blessings cheaper; cruel makes wrath cheaper and brings fearful offerings.
  • Last chances. When belief nearly dies, a crisis can bring a desperate prayer with just enough favor to answer it. A miracle at that moment wins far more belief than usual. Miss about three of these and you fade: the game is lost, and people carry on without you.
  • You start with about 50 favor and nobody knows you exist.

How they come to believe

  • Everything you do is marked as god-caused, but the people can't see that mark. They see a strange event.
  • Strange events create or strengthen a "sky one" node in the minds of people who see them, and link it to whatever they were doing just before.
  • If someone prayed and then it rained, that link grows. If enough people share it, praying for rain becomes a custom.
  • Whether you seem kind or cruel is a belief too. It builds from what follows your signs: help or harm. Later, that perception shapes which powers you get.

08 · How we'll know

Tests the milestone has to pass

  • Survivable but not safe. Left alone for 200 years across 50 random valleys, most bands survive; some die out.
  • Contingent. Without the god, pottery happens in some runs and not others, and when it happens it comes by at least three different routes.
  • Steerable, not instant. Using powers, you can reliably reach a chosen tile in a few generations, but you can't force it in a season.
  • Misread. Beliefs about the god form on their own, and some of them are wrong.
  • Loss. In some runs, a skill dies with the last person who knew it.
  • Explainable. For any action, the log can say why that person chose it.
  • Readable. The chronicle output reads like a story, e.g. "Year 34: Ren's granddaughter is the last who can make pots."
